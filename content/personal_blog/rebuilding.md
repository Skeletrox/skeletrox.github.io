I started a new job this week at Google. It is exciting, moving to a new company
that is seen as "the" place to work. Lunches, perks, gyms, swag, snacks, and
apparently what I have heard is "a good work environment". Coming from AWS, that
bar is a tripping hazard in hell.

However, after five years of building a community at AWS that I was a
part of, leaving it was not easy. I had a chance to work with some incredible
people, and while I am excited about my future at Google, I will always cherish
the time I had at AWS. I'll miss a lot of people there, and I wish them all the best.

I don't mean to use the word incredible in only a professional context. There have 
been folks who were brilliant technically but were never the ones you could be
friends with. There were folks who were friendly but did not really make your
life better. And then there were folks who were absolute treasures - folks you
could talk endlessly to, and then they'd have your back in the code review. They
were the incredible ones.

And every day, at 12:00 PM, you'd go for lunch with the same group of folks. This
was a time of reflection, of talking about your hobbies, your problems, your
successes, and everything in between. It's this camaraderie I'll miss the most.
You'd rant about customers to your skip manager, who would talk about stories of
his own. You'd talk about video games with an intern who was just starting out,
and listen to him talk about how excited he was about his future. Someone would
talk about their hike. Another about a new lens they installed on their camera.
A senior engineer would talk about their kids' lives, their schools, and give
some advice on real estate.

These conversations are invaluable. Young engineers get to listen to the stories
of the ones that came before them, absorb so much information, and build a sense
of community. They know they belong. They bring in insights and fresh perspectives
from a world outside the workplace. Senior engineers get more opportunities to
mentor, share their experiences, and see how the life they grew up in differs
from that of the new kids. Everyone gets to be friends, and at the very least,
they get to be peers.

I've been on both sides of this equation. As a new joiner, the sheer amount of
information you have to absorb is staggering. Not just the technical details,
but the company culture, the people, the way things are done. Lunchtime chats
are a godsend. You get to ask questions you'd be too embarrassed to ask in a
meeting, hear about other people's experiences, and just get a sense of the
place. It's how you find your footing, how you build relationships, and how you
discover that you're not the only one who's struggling to understand the
codebase.

As a senior engineer, it's easy to get caught up in the day-to-day grind. You
forget what it's like to be new, to be unsure of yourself. Lunchtime chats are a
reminder of that. You get to see the fresh perspectives, the enthusiasm, the
excitement of people who are just starting out. It's refreshing. It's grounding.
And it's a reminder of why you started doing this in the first place.

## New Beginnings

When you walk into a new place, the whole paradigm shifts. You go from being the
centerpiece of the group to a new guy finding his bearings in a room full of
strangers, navigating different internal tools that are second nature to others,
and that all-familiar feeling of "I don't belong here" creeps in. But then, you
know that the new guys in your old team felt the same way. You were the one that
took them out to lunch, made a joke about the food and the operational burden,
and genuinely tried to make them feel at home.

Of course, rebuilding these bonds takes time. You have to put yourself out there,
initiate conversations, and make an effort to connect with people. You talk to
people, make an effort, and some of them will be cool, some might not be, and some
will be absolute treasures. The counterpart to that is to be inclusive, to make
the new guy feel welcome (and not a burden) and to include them in your jokes, your
conversations, and your life.

This was my plan. However, after a few days, I realized that this might not be
as straightforward as I had hoped.

## Social and Cultural Gravity

It is well known that we look to familiarity when we are in a new place. Folks
who have a semblance of an idea who you are, and what you do, tend to be the ones
you gravitate towards. This does not mean that you need to
immediately drop all sense of self and turn into a thermometer, but rather show
up respectfully, and make an effort to get to know people.

Yet, there are times where the gravity that draws you in leads you to the exact
characteristics that make you feel like you don't belong, and the ones that were
the reason you developed your internal escape velocity to be someone entirely new.
To an outside observer, you're gravitating towards home. To you, you're finding
the same gravitational pull that you genuinely detested in the past.

I want to talk about this whole "gravity" situation. The team at AWS that I was in
had a shared gravity of being self-deprecating, of making fun of the system we
built (and ourselves), of complaining about the customers (and ourselves). This
was not a bad thing, and was not exclusionary by any means. You could be anyone,
whether you were a new grad, a tenured principal engineer, an intern, someone
who was quiet or someone who was loud, someone who was an extrovert or someone who
was an introvert, someone who was a people-pleaser or someone who was brutally
honest, someone who had been at AWS for a month or someone who had been at AWS for
10 years.

But what mattered the most was that you were welcome. You'd have Arabs talking to
Europeans talking to Indians talking to Australians... all respectful and learning
from each other.
Stereotypes were laughed about, dismantled, and used to further build a sense of
camaraderie. A Greek person would have tried a masala dosa the previous day, while
an Indian was noting down a tzatziki recipe which the Syrian and the Turk had
embellished with their own twists, each claiming their way was the right way. The
American from rural Ohio would have tried a Chinese place recommended by someone from
Guangzhou the previous week, and his life would have changed since. Someone else
was a fan of Georgian music. None of us were from Georgia.

Your senior engineer would discuss a French arthouse movie that you would not be
sure you would even understand, but it would spark conversations about art, 
culture, and life in general. Someone would do the 6-7 when it came up organically.
The new hire would laugh. You'd groan. The principal engineer would be clueless,
but you would ask them to talk to their children about it, silently laughing about
the chaos that'd unfold in their house later.

This was the norm. There was no pressure to fit in, to be someone you're not. 
You could be yourself, and you would be accepted. That is the gravity that I was
talking about. This is the gravity that makes any place feel like home.

## Why this is important to me

Being a treasure of a person (or a half
decent one at that), requires explicit effort. This means being welcoming, being
open-minded, and making an effort to put yourself in the other person's shoes.

We had a strict no politics rule at lunch, for example, unless the issue was very
broad-reaching or already in the news. If it did not matter to any of us, and was
just folks joking about the political climate, then it was fine. Otherwise, it was
not allowed. You did not have to feel unwelcome because you had a different political
viewpoint.

So a well-defined social contract that allowed people of different walks of life to
gravitate towards is something that I find incredibly important to build a good
social environment, whether it's in a workplace, in a community, or in any group of
people. For me, especially, existing gravitational centers that you would normally
find (race, ethnicity, religion, gender, etc.) are alienating enough. The hallmark
of a true leader is the ability to build such a community.

The reason is because I grew up as someone who never really "belonged". Growing
up in Bengaluru was interesting. My native language is Tamil, but the language
of Bengaluru is Kannada. My parents were Tamil, but my mom grew up here and still
speaks better formal Kannada than I do. I studied in a Kendriya Vidyalaya, where
we had students from all over the country. Most of us spoke English. My mother
herself was an English teacher, and we had excellent teachers who made sure that
we did not let our backgrounds define us. Some preferred Hindi. We were able to
code switch effortlessly between English, Hindi and Kannada.

For the parts of my life outside this environment, it has been a bit of a struggle.
I found myself too Tamilian to be called a Kannadiga, and vice-versa. It did not
help that my preferred language of speech was English. Not that I was pretentious
or anything, but the cosmopolitan environment of Bengaluru (thanks to our education
system) meant that most of us were multi-lingual. English was often the neutral
ground. Not for cousins of mine who grew up in parts of India where the local one
was the only language spoken, and English was seen as a language of someone who
thought they were better than everyone else.

That did not mean that I had no support system. Quite the opposite actually. The
friends I had in Bengaluru are some of the best people I have ever met. I had
cousins and relatives who genuinely appreciated me for who I was. They'd bring
Shakespeare. They'd discuss geopolitics with me when I was 13. They made me want
to be like them if I were to be in their positions.

It was a mixed bag growing up in India, but moving to the US made it a superpower.
My familiarity with social diversity allowed me to blend in well. My ability to
destroy preconcieved notions about who I was made a lot of people respect me, and
open themselves up to me. I had a childhood where I had to adapt. Now, it was
making me someone people wanted to know more about. I had to have an open mind
when dealing with people back home, and now that allowed me to be more open to
people, and understand different viewpoints and perspectives.

## Cliques and Solo Acts

Now we go to cliques. We almost always form cliques if left to our own devices.
That is human nature. It is up to you to recognize it, and actively work against
it, to ensure that everyone feels included. And unfortunately, I have seen a lot
of people fail at this. I saw it once at AWS, but luckily I had enough tenure by
then to just ignore the clique and their behavior. Then I saw it again last week.
And this time, I did not have that luxury. So yeah. Smile and wave boys, smile and
wave.

Let me bring up a recent conversation. Everything is anonymized, unless you know
who you are. I go out to lunch
with folks I don't really know well. You're new, and you're trying to make a good
impression. Someone comes up to me and starts talking in language X. I politely
say "I don't speak X" with a smile. They switch to English with me, and ask where
I'm from. "Bengaluru" I say. "Oh, so you don't speak X?". X is not even the
local language, but it is a language spoken by a significant minority in the city.
My school friends who spoke X spoke Kannada with me. The tone implies an expectation
that I should speak X, a point of contention for local Kannadigas who're probably
already angry that Bengaluru is not as Kannada-focused as it used to be.

Be that as it may, I don't care. I grew up in a city where people spoke many languages,
and I was exposed to many languages. I am proud of my background, and I will not
let anyone shame me for it. My kannadiga friends taught me Kannada when I asked them to.
I did not feel bad about it then, and I don't feel bad about it now. I teach my wife
Kannada now, and we enjoy practicing it and listening to gossip from strangers
who have no idea we understand them. Same thing with my friends and Tamil. No
jugment, and our backgrounds are diverse, and we add our unique flavors to the
stew that is life.

But a baseline was established in the conversation. I am from Bengaluru. Not from
X-Land. X-Landers should understand X, and if they don't, it's on them. Not me.
That's when I realized the problem was not with me or my background. It was with
the expectation set by my interlocutor. It's like going to a Sushi place and being
angry that they don't serve pasta. That's not on the restaurant. If you want pasta,
respectfully tell the Sushi place that you are looking for pasta, and if they don't
have it, enjoy the sushi, or excuse yourself and go find a place that serves
pasta. I have had friends do that to me multiple times. I don't mind. It's human
nature to not have the energy to accommodate someone new, and polite excusion
is something I've never had an issue with.

But yes, back to the conversation. The interlocutor and I continue the conversation
in English, and it's all fun and games until their friend comes along. They join us
and start speaking in X. I jump in front of the conversation and say, "Hi, nice to
meet you. I'm from Bengaluru. It's a pleasure." Not a single word of that was in X.
I think enough context has been established. If I knew X, I'd have spoken in X.
I get a couple of sentences answered before it's X again.

More folks join. It is now evident that I stick out like a sore thumb. They try
to make some conversation, and ask what languages I speak. "English, Kannada, and
Tamil" I reply. "Well you gotta learn X if you're in software engineering. We're
everywhere!" one of them chimes in, with a smile on their face. 

This conversation happened in the heart of Silicon Valley. Let that sink in. I
laugh, but my brain fires up. I speak 4 languages fluently. I speak
enough Spanish to get by in most of Latin America, and I pick up languages quickly.
I learnt French in high school and German for fun. I learnt Sanskrit for school.
And here I am, being told that I need to learn X in a job that does not require it.
A job where my interviews were in English, and all my work and designs are in
English. Makes you wonder. The statement was said again, with the same reasoning.
Then the conversation is back to X while I process WTF just happened.

Most of us come here with an expectation of English fluency and it being the
lingua franca of the tech industry. Which it is, for the most part. At least in
Big Tech in Silicon Valley. But every now and then, you see stuff like this. You
see ignorance masquerading as banter, and everything being twisted to make you
feel like you're the odd one out.

I laughed it off, but I couldn't stop thinking about it. It is a subtle exclusion.
It is a subtle way of saying that you do not belong here. And the worst part? For
an outsider, I and the chauvinist look the same. We're most likely to be grouped
together. I don't blame them, I guess. It's not like it's easy to tell who's
who. And ironically, Bengaluru is where most of them would want to end up in
should they move to India.

But the problem is, that each time I open reddit or blind, I see stuff from folks
that aren't Indian talking about the exact same thing. And each time I went to
the lunch table in Redshift, I'd see that we'd managed to overcome it together.
What I appreciate now is that we opened up about it and talked about it. We made
conscious efforts to not exclude each other. It seemed effortless then, but now I
realize that it needed directed intent.

I'd argue that calling out exclusionary behavior in the diaspora is not a betrayal
of your roots. It's a demand that we hold ourselves to the high standards we expect
from everyone else.

### Solo Acts

Solo acts does not mean going Rambo in this context. I call it a way for me to
assert my ownership over my identity by being comfortable with being solo. Doing
so means that you can make decisions that are true to yourself without external
validation. It's freeing.

For example, when I was in Redshift, all I did was just sit at the table. Different
folks would come and go. Most of the time, I'd just sit there and eat, nodding along to their 
conversations. Sometimes I'd interject with a word or two, sometimes I wouldn't. It didn't
matter. I was comfortable with being solo. I was comfortable with my identity.
I didn't feel the need to change that for anyone. And that was the way in. The
conversations would slowly morph into topics that everyone could relate to, and
in a few months, we had folks making connections that lasted for years.

The massiveness of the Google campus also helps. You can be solo for a few
months, and not even notice. You can go from one building to the next, and never
run into the same people twice. It's a great way to be solo, without feeling
lonely. Maybe you might find someone new, maybe not. The point being, you are
comfortable enough with yourself to not feel the need for validation from others,
or for the need to force others to fall into your clique.

### Bringing it all together

I spent a lot of time on cliques in this article because it is the main reason I
felt the need to go solo on my rebuilding journey. However, what triggered the
need to write such a long article in the first place was that in a time of
growing xenophobia and hatred, we often forget that the acts we do and the words
we say directly feed the thoughts and mindset of the accusers. Whether it is an
ingrained inability to accommodate someone new, or a person's inherent biases and
prejudices, these actions trigger an echo chamber of hate and division in the
world. When I heard the words "You need to learn X if you're in software
engineering" I was triggered because it's the same narrative you hear on the news
about why people from certain places disproportionately dominate certain fields,
more often negatively than not.

Not only does this sentiment fuel the fires of xenophobia and hate, it also
disproportionately affects people who are already marginalized, and minimizes the
efforts of people who are fighting for change despite being part of the perceived
dominant group. Reactionaries to the accusers, who (like a broken clock being
right twice a day) may correctly point out the hypocrisy of the situation, often
disregard the validity of the original accusation, which is that such behavior
is exclusionary and goes against the very fabric of what the community is supposed
to be.

And when left unchecked, it leads to the unbridled corruption and a feeling of
ennui and resentment that you see in Reddit and Blind. And when you have folks who
openly admit to being part of cliques and wanting to propagate them, while you
did not travel 8000 miles just to be a part of a clique that is not only one that
you'd find back home, but one that is still exclusionary to you - you just get fed
up.

I believe that saying this does not make me a sepoy. I know that some of this was
a survival mechanism for some. It's just that there is no need for exclusion in this
day and age, especially when you're thriving and not surviving any more. And me
calling my countrymen out for this sentiment doesn't make me anti-Indian. In fact,
I would argue that it's anti-nationalist to accept a broken system and propagate
it forward, especially when it is completely at odds with what makes India, India.
Or what makes the United States, the United States.

Going solo is a part of the process, I guess. A process I find all too familiar.
But it is also a process I find liberating, because there are no expectations of you.
And if you don't find the community you were looking for, you can always look
for it elsewhere, or build you own, with the right level of access to the right
people.

## Rebuilding

I am going to address the title of the post. Rebuilding oneself is a continuous
process. It is not something that happens overnight, or something that happens
only once. It happens in stages, and it happens due to a multitude of factors.
Yet, we always seem to expect help and external validation in this process. Instead,
the only question one must ask themselves is - "how can I be comfortable with
myself? And do I need external validation for the person I want to be?" The answer
likely is no. Because if you do, you are moulding yourself using the blueprints of
someone else's identity, and you end up losing your own.

Rebuilding oneself on one's own terms requires a high degree of introspection, an
ability to ask oneself the hard questions, and an innate sense of condifence that
comes with competence. Or competence might just follow if you're confident and willing
to ask yourself questions. It's a chicken and egg problem you can ponder while
you have lunch in a new cafeteria, alone.

## In conclusion

Don't be exclusionary in your interactions. Don't be exclusionary in your identity.
Be yourself, and be open to others. And don't feed the negative stereotypes, please.
It is hard to be better than this, but it isn't impossible. Don't make it harder
for the next person.

In the end, remember - you belong to yourself first, and then to the world. And
nobody can take that away from you, or hate you when you respectfully show up
for yourself.
